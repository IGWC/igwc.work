What does it mean to Know Your Rights?

In response to organizing across the country in defense of international students, the Federal Government has begun to reinstate hundreds of illegally-terminated SEVIS records. University workers and students showed the power of solidarity, preparation, and mass action. This mass solidarity is the strongest form of defense against the chaotic attacks on our rights, our labor, and our friends.

Solidarity goes hand-in-hand with trust. In a deeply uncertain environment, it's more important than ever to share grounded, precise information/resources rather than spread rumors and panic.

Knowing your rights isn't just about upholding rights as an abstract cause. It's about the concrete steps and preparations you can make if you're faced with a tough situation. In general, when interacting with police, there are a few things to remember:

  1. Never answer any questions without a lawyer

    You are only legally required to give your name, address, and birthday when asked by the police. Don't answer any other questions without a lawyer.

  2. Do not open the door

    If the police knock on your door, ask to see and inspect the warrant—have them slide the warrant under the door, or take a picture through the window. Do not open the door without checking all of the information on the warrant to ensure it is correct. Opening the door even slightly allows the police to claim you consented to the search.

  3. Do not agree to a search

    Any search warrant will have specific items that are subject to search. Anything that is not in clear view is not subject to search without cause. While border crossings have slightly different rules, police cannot search without cause or consent. If you are preparing to travel, research the specific regulations or reach out to BloomAIR@proton.me.

There are a few important phrases to remember when interacting with any police:

  • "I will not speak without my attorney present."

  • "I choose to remain silent."

  • "Am I being detained / Am I free to leave?"

  • "Can I see your ID and business card?"

  • "I do not agree to this search."

If you are an international student, one concrete action to protect yourself is to make sure you've prepared the following materials:

  • Notarized letter of "power of attorney" for your home, your pay, your car, your children, and your bank accounts

  • Signed G-28 to be used by an immigration attorney

  • A signed DHS Privacy Waiver Authorizing Disclosure to a Third Party to give someone who is not an attorney access to information about you

  • Birth certificates for your children if you’re a parent

  • Documentation proving how long you have been in the US (especially if you have been here for more than 2 or 10 years)

  • Proof of education (if you studied in the US)

  • Medical information for you, your children, or any other dependents, including a HIPPA agreement to get medical records

  • Your passport or other identifications; and any other immigration or legal document

If you need help putting together these materials, reach out to BloomAIR@proton.me.

More details, guidance, and a list of legal support services are available in at linktr.ee/btownrights4intlstudents and in a presentation put together by the Bloomington Alliance for Immigrant Rights.
