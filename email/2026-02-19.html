Cost of Living in Bloomington is rising. Wages aren’t (except after strikes).

● Bryce Greene, Informatics
This story initially appeared in the IGWC Field Report.

It’s nothing new that the cost of living is getting out of hand, but it is important to reiterate the role of the union in addressing the crisis. As of today, according to the MIT calculator, it takes $43,605 annually for a single person. For people with a child to live, that number skyrockets to $80,853. Meanwhile the graduate student stipend sits at $24,000 or just 55% of the living wage (30% or less if you have children).

In 2022, stipends after paying student fees to the university were under $14,000 with some students making around $12,000 after fees. The IGWC strike shut down university classes for four weeks as we demanded a living wage and recognition. Though the strike ended without an official resolution or recognition, the impacts of worker power were clear. Before the beginning of the next semester, the university raised graduate stipends to $22,000.

When announcing this rise, the university attributed it to the work of a task force and personal action taken by the Provost and President, completely omitting the role of the IGWC. Why would he or anyone else want to acknowledge where serious power lies? But it was clear that it was our pressure that forced the university’s hand.  Recognizing the inadequacy of the raise and the continued lack of recognition, IGWC went on a three day strike in the Spring of 2024. In each successive summer, the university responded by adding $1000 to the minimum stipend, bringing us to today's minimum.

IU’s cost of living crisis will continue to get worse based on trends in the last decade. Since 2021, when the cost of living was just $28,787, the stipends covered just 54% of the cost of living in Bloomington, close to today's post-strike percentage of 55%.

To even keep pace with the dramatic increase in the cost of living, it took dramatic action from graduate workers to put the issue front and center. As the cost of living crisis intensifies, that kind of organizing is precisely what we need now. In just a decade, Bloomington’s cost of living has increased from just $17,139 by over 250%. Housing is one of the most important of all these. All indications point to this trend continuing.

The lessons from this are clear. IU has the resources to support their robust educational mission, but they choose not to. The only thing pushing them to maintain standards of graduate worker conditions is our continued militancy. It would of course be preferable to be a recognized union, and have a de jure seat at the table where these issues are decided. But until Indiana University decides to recognize us, our very survival relies on collective organizing.
